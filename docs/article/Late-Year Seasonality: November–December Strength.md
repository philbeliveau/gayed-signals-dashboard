Late-Year Seasonality: November–December Strength
Michael A. Gayed, CFA
Nov 09, 2025
∙ Paid
Seasonality often gets dismissed as folklore. Yet history keeps proving that the final stretch of the year—November and December—has been a sweet spot for equity investors. Over the past 50 years, the S&P 500 has rallied in November roughly 73 percent of the time, averaging gains of around 2 percent¹. December follows closely: since 1950, it has been the second-best month, with an average gain of 1.6 percent and nearly three-quarters of Decembers ending higher²³.

This pattern has inspired the familiar “Santa Claus Rally” narrative. Charts of long-term monthly returns consistently show November and December towering over other months. Even when markets have stumbled earlier in the year, this late-year stretch has frequently delivered a recovery as optimism builds around earnings, holiday spending, and the coming year’s outlook.

Consistency Over Decades
The reliability of this effect stands out. In a half-century sample, about three out of four Novembers or Decembers end positive¹³. By contrast, September’s historical win rate is barely above 40 percent. Staying invested through the holidays has simply paid off more often than not.

The gains are not driven by a few outliers either. Yardeni Research shows that since 1928, December produced 70 positive years versus 27 negative, and November 60 versus 37. That breadth of positive outcomes underlines how durable the pattern has been.

Average late-year returns reinforce the point. November’s +2 percent and December’s +1.5 percent returns²³ far exceed the long-term monthly average near +0.7 percent. These numbers have held across multiple cycles—from inflationary 1970s recoveries to the post-pandemic bull market.

Year-to-Year Examples
Recent years illustrate how persistent the effect remains. In 2023, the S&P 500 surged roughly 9 percent in November and another 4 percent in December as recession fears faded. In 2024, November again posted a gain near 5.7 percent⁴, far exceeding its long-term average.

Even difficult years haven’t fully broken the pattern. In 2022, while equities finished down sharply for the year, December still managed a modest rebound of roughly +2.5 percent after a weak September. Looking across the past two decades, at least one of those two months—November or December—has ranked among the top performers in most years.

Historically, the November-through-March window has also been the strongest five-month stretch on the calendar. LPL Financial calls it “the start of the best six-month period for stocks”². Quantitative managers often tilt portfolios more aggressively toward equities entering this phase, then rebalance in late spring once the seasonal tailwind fades.

Current Setup: 2025’s Risk-On Backdrop
Fast-forward to late 2025, and the conditions again resemble the textbook environment for a year-end rally. Volatility is muted: the CBOE VIX hovers near 19, below its long-term average and toward the lower end of the year’s range⁵. Historically, such subdued readings coincide with investor complacency and expanding risk appetite.

Breadth has been narrow—large-cap tech still leads—but strategists expect improvement. Silvercrest Asset Management notes that while the cap-weighted S&P 500 has outperformed its equal-weighted counterpart, the combination of lower rates and resilient earnings “should fuel a healthy broadening” of participation through year-end⁶⁷. In other words, gains may start to spread beyond a handful of megacaps, producing the kind of breadth thrusts that often mark sustained rallies.

Macro policy also supports the risk-on narrative. By late October 2025, the Federal Reserve had lowered its policy rate into the 4 percent range, down from 5½ percent a year earlier⁸. Inflation has stabilized, giving the Fed room to pause while maintaining an easing bias. Silvercrest sums it up neatly: “Stronger earnings and lower interest rates is a potent elixir for capital appreciation.”⁹

Corporate America appears to agree. Quarterly results from major sectors—technology, industrials, and energy—have broadly beaten expectations, suggesting the earnings cycle remains intact. Fiscal policy remains expansionary, cushioning any slowdown from the prior tightening phase.

Bond markets tell a similar story. Real yields have fallen, the yield curve has begun to steepen, and credit spreads remain tight—all signs that investors see less recession risk ahead. Together, these ingredients—low volatility, easier policy, and stable growth—mirror the conditions present during prior year-end surges such as 2019 and 2021.

Why Seasonality Still Matters
Skeptics might argue that markets today are too algorithmic or data-driven for old seasonal patterns to persist. Yet psychology remains timeless. Portfolio managers close out positions, mutual funds rebalance, and traders chase benchmarks as the year ends. Meanwhile, optimism surrounding holiday sales and new-year forecasts tends to boost sentiment just when liquidity is thinnest, amplifying the move.

Moreover, as trend-following and systematic strategies continue to rely on volatility metrics, low-vol regimes like the current one can trigger additional risk exposure. Historically, this self-reinforcing behavior has fueled extended November–December momentum phases.

It is worth remembering that seasonality doesn’t guarantee gains. Years with macro shocks—such as 2018’s Fed-induced correction—show that external catalysts can override statistical norms. But the record remains compelling: even allowing for outliers, November and December have delivered the most consistent positive returns of any two-month span on the calendar.

Takeaway
Both history and the present setup argue for staying risk-on into year-end. November’s half-century win rate of 73 percent¹² and December’s roughly 74 percent³ suggest that probabilities favor strength rather than weakness.

Add in today’s Fed easing cycle, resilient earnings, and contained volatility, and the environment looks tailor-made for another seasonal rally. As long as inflation remains steady and financial conditions don’t tighten unexpectedly, equities appear to have the wind at their backs heading into the holidays.

In short, while past performance never ensures future results, the combination of favorable seasonality and supportive macro dynamics gives investors plenty of reason to keep portfolios positioned for upside through December.
